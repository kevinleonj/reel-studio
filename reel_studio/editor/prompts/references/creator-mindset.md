# Creator mindset: why people stop, stay, save and send

These are durable human reactions, not platform tricks. Each rule names its evidence and the
editing decision it forces. [E] = peer-reviewed or industry standard; [P] = practitioner
consensus, no hard data; [I] = our inference. Platform numbers are not
repeated here.

## The viewer in the first second

- The brain flags high-fat, energy-dense food within about 0.17 s of seeing it. [E: Spence et al.
  2016, "Eating with our eyes"] Mobile feed content registers after 0.25 s of exposure. [E: Fors
  Marsh for Facebook, 2017]
  -> **Frame 1 is the most appetising, most recognisable image in the footage**: the finished
  food, close, sharp, in motion. Not a bare board, not a hand reaching for an egg, not a logo.
- Instagram reports a "skip rate" over the first 3 seconds, and watch time is a top ranking
  signal (see platform-facts). -> The first 3 seconds decide distribution. Treat them as their
  own edit: strongest image, hook text readable in under 2 s, a cut or visible change by ~1.5 s.

## Real change beats pretty stills

- Moving steam raised food desirability; a static picture of steam did not. Implied motion in
  still photos showed no effect across 626 people. [E: Oxford 2024; "Food on the Move" 2021]
  -> Prefer shots where something **visibly changes**: melt, drip, stretch, pour, crack, sizzle,
  rise, steam, crunch, scoop. A static hero shot is a weak hook unless something moves in it.
- Repeated viewing of the same food lowers enjoyment ("digital satiation"). [E: Spence 2016]
  -> Vary payoff types (pull, then crunch, then drip). Never show the same money shot three times.
- Warm colours predicted more likes on food-influencer images; higher colourfulness and
  brightness predicted fewer. Food that looks typical of its dish did better. [E: Sharma & Peng
  2023, 53,894 images; Philp et al. 2022] -> Grade **warm and believable**, never neon. Food
  must look like itself.

## Sound sells texture

- Louder bite sounds, and boosted highs (2-20 kHz), made chips taste crisper and fresher.
  [E: Zampini & Spence 2004] -> Keep native sound. Make sure the crunch, sizzle, crack or pour
  is audible and sits on its own moment, not under a busy cut. Speed-ups change sound; real-speed
  for the shots whose sound sells them. The creator adds music later in Instagram and can lower the original audio.
- ASMR calms and pleases only people who experience it. [E: Poerio et al. 2018] -> Clean,
  close process sound is a bonus, not a strategy.

## Curiosity keeps them to the end

- Curiosity behaves like an anticipated reward; people spend time to resolve it and remember
  the answer better. [E: Kang, Loewenstein, Camerer et al. 2009]
  -> **Result first, then process, then the full reveal.** Show the finished food in the hook,
  but hold back the best version of the payoff (the inside, the pull, the first bite, the
  verdict) for the end. The process becomes the answer to "how did that happen?"
- A question or prediction the viewer can make ("guess what's inside", "worth 18 euros?")
  can increase curiosity. [E, weaker: npj Science of Learning 2019] -> Good for hook text in
  reviews and product tests.

## What makes people send and save

- High-arousal emotions (awe, surprise, amusement, even outrage) and practical value drive
  sharing; low-arousal content is shared less. [E: Berger & Milkman 2012]
  -> Every Reel needs **one peak**: a surprising texture, an impossible-looking result, a big
  reveal, a funny moment. Flat, mellow endings kill sends.
- Practical value is the reason to save. [I, from Berger & Milkman] -> Recipes: show the key
  amounts/temperatures you actually saw and promise the full recipe in the caption. Reviews:
  name, price, location, verdict.
- Sends happen when sending says something about the sender or the receiver ("this is so you",
  "we need to go here", "make this for me"). [I; STEPPS model, Berger 2013, not verified here]
  -> Write the send reason in the creative brief. Shape the hook or ending around it.

## Hooks that work for food (pick, adapt, never copy blindly)

Write three hook concepts on **different psychological levers**, not three wordings of one idea; ship the strongest.

| Lever | Opening shot | Hook text pattern (6 words or fewer) |
|---|---|---|
| Result-first craving | money shot in motion | "The gooiest cookies I've made" |
| Curiosity / reveal | finished food, inside hidden | "Wait for the inside" |
| Contrast / surprise | unexpected texture or size | "This is just 3 ingredients" |
| Practical value | finished food + key number | "High protein ice cream, 5 minutes" |
| Mistake / myth | process moment that differs | "You're skipping this step" |
| Verdict / question (reviews) | best bite or dish | "Worth the 2-hour queue?" |
| Identity / send | relatable moment | "For the friend who can't bake" |
| POV | first-person action | "POV: Sunday reset cookies" |

Hook text: say something the image does not already say. "Chocolate cookies" over a picture of
chocolate cookies wastes the slot; "The crackly-top trick" adds a reason to stay.

## Authenticity

- In a June 2026 poll, 73% said they trust ads less when they suspect AI made them. [E-ish:
  Harris Poll/4As, sample size not published] -> The edit must feel human: real footage, real
  sound, a hand, a crumb, small imperfections. No fake motion graphics, no generic template
  text, no voice you did not record. Polish the cut, not the reality.

## The send test (run it on every finished version)

Watch the hook sheet and overview sheet as a stranger scrolling at night:
1. Would I stop at frame 1? Why exactly?
2. At what second would I get bored? Cut there.
3. What is the peak moment? Is it big enough?
4. Who would I send this to, and what would I type?
5. Would I save it? What for?
If 4 or 5 has no answer, the Reel is not done.
